Knob-and-Tube & Home Insurance:
The 2026 Reality

The single most common "deal-breaker" on a century home isn't the roof — it's the wiring from 1912. Here's what insurers actually ask, what a rewire costs, and how to buy the house anyway.

What knob-and-tube actually is

Knob-and-tube (K&T) was the first standardized electrical system in US homes — two open copper wires run through porcelain knobs and tubes, ungrounded, usually with no junction boxes. It was code-compliant in 1912. In 2026 it is, for many insurers, a refusal letter.

What underwriters ask

  • Is the K&T still in service, or just present? Present-but-disconnected is generally fine.
  • Was it modified or spliced recently? Improper splices to Romex are the real fire story.
  • What's the panel? K&T-era panels (and Federal Pacific/Zinsco of any era) are instant flags.
  • Is the attic insulation covering K&T? Covered wires can overheat — a common reason for refusal after a retrofit.

The real cost to fix it

The strategic point: a rewire is a before-walls-close, before-insulation, before-anything-pretty job. It belongs in the day 15-30 window of the 90-day sequence — never after the plaster is done.

When you're shopping

  • Get a licensed electrician's evaluation quote in writing and use it as a price-reduction lever.
  • Ask the seller who carriers have insured it — a "most carriers decline" history is a known quantity to price.
  • Specialty insurers (and some state JUA programs) will bind coverage with a mitigation letter and a dated electrician's report.
  • If you're keeping K&T short-term: confirm it's unmodified, uninsulated, and on a 15-amp circuit — then plan the rewire into year one.
