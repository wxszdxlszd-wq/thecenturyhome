13 Old-House Renovation Mistakes That Cost Real Money

Five of these break most first-time century-home owners; the other eight are quieter but just as expensive. Every number below is a 2025–26 US market figure.

1. Tearing Out Original Woodwork "For a Fresh Start"

This is the one owners regret most. Original trim, doors, and built-ins are the single biggest value-and-character asset of an old house. Replacing them with new millwork costs $3,000–$8,000 for a typical house — and the result looks like every other flipped property on Zillow. Restoring original woodwork runs about 20% of replacement cost and keeps the house honest. If a contractor suggests "just rip it out," get a second opinion from a preservation-minded craftsman.

  • Original fir doors: restore with a hardwax oil finish, not replacement
  • Built-in cabinets: strip paint, tighten joinery, re-hang the hardware
  • Window trim: re-glaze and repaint — never replace
  • Restore vs. replace: roughly 20% of full replacement cost

2. Patching Plaster With Drywall Mud

Old lime plaster is a flexible, breathable system. Drywall joint compound is stiff gypsum. Patch old plaster with joint compound and the crack comes back within a season — then you pay twice. The right fix uses plaster-compatible lime putty, plaster washers for loose keys, and patience. Whole-house plaster repair typically runs $4,000–$12,000; drywalling over everything costs $12,000–$20,000 and destroys the wall's acoustic quality and character.

  • Plaster washers for bowed or loose sections: $2–$5 per sq ft
  • Skim coat to preserve profile — the depth of original molding is part of the look
  • Drywall overlay: destroys the profile and kills resale character

3. Closing Walls Before the Wiring Is Solved

Knob-and-tube wiring was code-compliant in 1950. It is a fire hazard and an insurance red flag today (more on that in the K&T insurance guide). If you drywall over it to save money now, you'll pay $8,000–$20,000 to rip walls open later — or discover at resale that no carrier will insure the property. Rule: rewire before you close any wall. Same for galvanized plumbing, which rusts from the inside out whether or not you can see it.

  • Federal Pacific and Zinsco panels: immediate replacement, no exceptions
  • Galvanized supply lines: budget $4,000–$12,000 to repipe in PEX
  • Schedule MEP rough-in before any finish work begins

4. Replacing Original Windows Instead of Restoring Them

A house full of original wood sash windows is a feature, not a defect. Restoration (re-glazing, new sash cords, weatherstripping, storm panels) runs $200–$600 per window DIY. Replacement vinyl windows installed run $800–$1,200 each — and on an old house, they look wrong and kill resale appeal. Restored wood windows, properly weatherstripped and fitted with interior storm panels, perform within 5–10% of modern double-pane units.

  • Re-glazing compound: actual glazing putty, not caulk
  • Sash cord replacement: $60–$120 per window DIY
  • V-strip weatherstripping outperforms foam tape by 5× lifespan
  • Interior storm inserts: $250–$450 per window, no drilling

5. Demolishing Before You've Budgeted the Rebuild

Demo is the fun, cheap, satisfying part. It's also the moment owners blow their budget, because the rebuild always costs more than the wrecking ball promised. Old-house budgets need a 15–20% surprise reserve on top of real line items — not the 10% contractors quote for new builds. Every opened wall reveals something. A $180,000 full renovation should carry a $27,000–$36,000 reserve. Budget the rebuild before you swing the hammer.

  • Get 3 contractor bids before demolition, not after
  • Structural engineer assessment: $400–$800 — always worth it
  • Order materials 4–8 weeks ahead — supply delays cost more than mistakes

6. Skipping the Sewer Camera

Original clay or cast-iron drains are at or past end of life. Root intrusion, offset joints, and bellying are invisible without a camera. The check costs $300–$500; the surprise costs $8,000–$22,000. Camera the main line from clean-out to street before you own the problem — see check 1 of the pre-purchase guide.

7. Paying the Down Payment the Contractor Asks For

Never more than 10–15% down, milestone payments tied to inspected work, and the final 10% held until the punch list is complete. A contractor asking 40–50% up front is either in a cash-flow problem or planning to be. The full payment schedule with change-order clauses is in the Kit's contractor playbook.

8. Getting Two Bids Instead of Three

The range between the highest and lowest bid on a single old-house trade is often $4,000–$12,000. Two bids tell you the average of two guesses; three tell you the shape of the market. Same scope, same spec sheet, three contractors.

9. DIYing the Licensed Work

Electrical, structural, sewer, and gas are not "money-saving opportunities" — they're permit work. A failed inspection costs more than the pro ever would have (see the full DIY breakdown). Paint, tile, flooring, demo, and landscaping are where your hours actually compound.

10. Ignoring the Permit History

Un-permitted additions, kitchen remodels, or electrical work become your liability at resale — and some jurisdictions require retroactive compliance before allowing any new permit. The building-department call costs 30 minutes and can reveal $5,000–$40,000 in inherited problems. This is check 9 in the pre-purchase guide.

11. Disturbing Abatement Materials Without Testing

Pre-1978 paint, 9″ tile, popcorn ceilings, and old duct insulation are all testable for $50–$400. Disturbed without testing, the same materials become a stop-work order and a licensed-abatement line item of $2,200–$6,500. Test before you sand, scrape, or pry.

12. Sourcing New "Period-Look" Instead of Real Salvage

Reproduction hardware and "vintage-style" lighting cost 2–3× what genuine period pieces cost at salvage yards and estate sales — and they read as reproductions. The Kit's sourcing chapter lists six hunting grounds where real pieces run 30–60% of new.

13. Falling in Love Before the Audit

The most expensive mistake isn't a trade — it's making an offer before you know the water story, the systems story, and the character story. The 30-minute audit exists to be run at the showing, before your heart commits: it's free and printable. Every other mistake on this list is downstream of that one.

When each mistake bites 鈥?a timing map

Most owners make the mistakes in a predictable order, because renovation forces decisions in a predictable order. Knowing when each trap springs helps you stay ahead of it:

  • Week 0 鈥?the showing. Mistake 13 (falling in love before the audit). The cheapest moment to run the walkthrough is the moment you see the house.
  • Between offer and closing. Mistake 10 (skipping the permit history) and mistake 11 (untested abatement). Both are pre-closing information you can negotiate with 鈥?after closing, they are bills.
  • Contract signing. Mistakes 7 and 8 (payment terms and the two-bid trap). Both are decided in the hour you sign, and both are unfixable after.
  • Demo day. Mistake 5 (demolishing before budgeting the rebuild) and mistake 3 (closing walls before wiring). Demo is when budgets die; the rebuild is always more expensive than the wrecking ball promised.
  • Finishes. Mistakes 1, 2, 4, and 12 (woodwork, plaster, windows, sourcing). These are the ones owners regret most, because they are visible every day 鈥?and they were all reversible at week 0.

Notice the pattern: every mistake on this list is a decision made in one afternoon and paid for over three years. The audit, the sequence, and the vetting questions exist to move those afternoons earlier.
